*A technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools — and what I learned along the way.*

## What Is My Family's Complete Financial Picture — Right Now?

That question sounds simple. It isn't. And the reason it isn't simple is not laziness or bad habits — it's that the financial system in India is genuinely fragmented. Each platform has its own app, each investment type has its own rules, each currency requires its own conversion logic, and nobody has bothered to stitch it together for you because the industry makes money from fragmentation.

I'm a technical product manager. I started my career as a software engineer, moved into product management five years ago, and for the last few years I've been building software products — just not, usually, for myself.

So I built one for myself. And for my family.

The product is called **NEESH** — Never Ending Earnest Savings Hustle. Which is both an accurate description of what managing a family's finances feels like, and a reminder that the point is to keep going.

---

## Why Not Just Buy Something?

I looked. I spent time evaluating every personal finance product I could find designed for an Indian context.

The problems were consistent.

**Privacy.** Every serious financial aggregator requires connecting your accounts via credentials or third-party APIs. That means your financial data — all of it — lives on someone else's servers. I have family members whose financial data I am absolutely not putting in someone else's cloud.

**Subscription math.** A ₹500/month subscription over 50 years is ₹3,00,000. For software that might not exist in year 12. I own a Raspberry Pi 4. It costs ₹5,000. It can run Python.

**Scope mismatch.** The apps that handled mutual funds didn't handle RSUs properly. The apps that handled RSUs weren't designed for Indian tax rules. The apps built for Indian tax rules didn't know what an ESPP is. Nobody handled all eleven things I needed.

**Longevity.** I wanted something designed to last five decades — not something I'd need to migrate data out of when the startup ran out of runway.

So: self-hosted, Python, single SQLite file, runs on hardware I own, built to last as long as I maintain it.

---

## What I Actually Built

NEESH tracks **11 asset classes:**

- Direct equity (stocks)
- Mutual funds and ETFs
- RSUs, ESOPs, ESPPs — with perquisite tax and FX conversion
- Fixed deposits and Sovereign Gold Bonds
- Bank accounts with fund flow linking
- Provident Fund
- Loans

It handles **two currencies** (INR and USD), **live market data** from yfinance, AMFI, and RBI reference rates, **FIFO lot tracking** for capital gains classification, and **XIRR computation** using a Rust-based library.

It has an **AI-powered import pipeline** that parses Zerodha tradebooks, CAMS statements, and arbitrary broker formats using a three-tier model cascade — more on that in a later post.

It runs as a **single Python process** — one FastAPI server, one SQLite database in WAL mode, one file for backups. No Docker, no Redis, no PostgreSQL daemon. The entire stack fits on a Raspberry Pi.

It supports **up to 4 family members** per family group, with a combined family dashboard and role-based access (Owner, Editor, Viewer). My parents can see their portfolio. I can see mine. We can both see the family view.

**Phase 1 has been live for one month.** In that time I have never lost data, never paid for hosting, and never opened seven different apps to check my net worth. Phases 2 and 3 — expense tracking, loan simulation, and tax planning — are what I'm working on now.

# The First Mistake Every PM Makes When Building with AI (I Made It Too)

*Part 2 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

A product manager's first instinct, when presented with a powerful new tool, is to use it.

That instinct is wrong.

When I started building NEESH, I had a problem I understood well — my family's finances were scattered everywhere and I wanted a single dashboard to see all of it. I had a rough idea of what the product should do. And I had a capable AI tool sitting in front of me, ready to go.

So I gave it the idea. I described the concept. I asked it to help me build it.

The AI was helpful. It said yes, this sounds like a valuable problem to solve. It asked a few clarifying questions. It began generating suggestions.

And almost everything it generated was wrong — not because the AI was bad at its job, but because **I had given it nothing real to work with.**

> *The AI is not a mind reader. It works with what you give it. Vagueness in, plausible-sounding-but-wrong out.*

This is the first mistake. And it's the one that costs the most time, because by the time you realise it, you've often built something substantial in the wrong direction.

---

## What "Vague" Actually Looks Like

Here's what I gave the AI on day one, paraphrased:

*"I want to build a personal finance tracker that consolidates all my investments in one place. It should support mutual funds, stocks, fixed deposits, and maybe RSUs. I want to see my net worth."*

That sounds reasonable. It isn't.

What asset classes exactly? Stocks on which exchanges? RSUs in which currency, at which conversion rate, with what tax treatment? Net worth updated how often — real-time, daily, on demand? What happens when I sell something — does the holding disappear? What does an FD look like at maturity vs. premature withdrawal?

None of those questions were answered because I hadn't answered them for myself. The AI made assumptions. Some were reasonable. Most were incomplete. A few were subtly wrong in ways that wouldn't surface until Sprint 6 or 7, when I'd built on top of a foundation that had a crack in it.

The lesson isn't that AI is unreliable. The lesson is that **you have to clear your own head before you can direct an AI.** Think of it as a highly capable executor — not a creative partner who rescues vague ideas. It produces excellent output when given excellent input, and it produces plausible-sounding-but-wrong output when given fog.

---

## The Four Steps That Changed Everything

Once I understood the problem, I stopped talking to the AI and started thinking.

These four steps happened before any code was written, before any design documents were created, before I asked the AI to do anything except listen.

---

**Step 1 — Unconstrained Brainstorming**

I wrote down every feature I wanted. Every single one. No filtering, no self-editing, no "but is that realistic."

Investment tracking. Expense categorisation. AI-powered statement import. Loan simulation. Tax projections. WhatsApp notifications. Family accounts. Zerodha integration. Raspberry Pi hosting. Automated backups. Corporate action detection. FIFO lot tracking.

Everything. The rule was: no filtering during this phase.

This served a specific purpose. If you edit while you brainstorm, you kill ideas before you understand why you had them. I needed the full, unfiltered list before I could evaluate it.

---

**Step 2 — Ruthless Prioritisation**

Every item on the list went through one filter: *does this serve the core product vision?*

The core vision was specific: **one honest view of my family's complete financial picture, correct enough to make real decisions.**

Anything that didn't directly serve that was cut. Not because it was a bad idea — some of it was genuinely interesting — but because a product that tries to do everything does nothing well.

This is harder than it sounds. You've just spent an hour generating ideas. Some of them are exciting. The discipline is cutting them anyway.

---

**Step 3 — Must Have vs. Nice to Have**

What survived prioritisation went into an impact × effort matrix.

High impact, reasonable effort → Phase 1.
Lower impact or high complexity → Phase 2 or 3.

This gave me a Phase 1 that was ambitious enough to be genuinely useful but scoped tightly enough to actually be buildable. The 11 asset classes, the AI import pipeline, the family accounts, the live market data — all Phase 1. The loan simulation engine, the tax projections, the expense tracking — Phase 2. The AI investment planning assistant — Phase 3.

The matrix wasn't complicated. A simple grid on paper. But having it forced every item through the same evaluation, which meant the scope decisions were consistent rather than arbitrary.

---

**Step 4 — Full User Journeys**

This was the step I almost skipped. It was also the most important one.

For every feature in Phase 1, I wrote the complete user journey. Not "the user can add a transaction." The actual flow:

*The user opens the app. They navigate to the equity section. They click Add Transaction. A form appears. What fields are on it? What happens when they submit? What does the holdings page look like after? What happens if the symbol isn't found in the market data provider? What happens if they try to sell more shares than they own?*

Every scenario. Every edge case I could imagine.

By the time I finished the user journeys, I had answered most of the questions the AI would have needed to make good decisions. I had also found a dozen issues in my own thinking — contradictions between features, missing flows, assumptions I'd made that didn't hold up when I worked through the detail.

---

## Then — and Only Then — I Went Back to the AI

I gave it the consolidated planning document. Everything from the brainstorm, the prioritisation decisions, the MoSCoW classification, and the full user journeys.

But I gave it one specific instruction: **evaluate this, don't build it.**

*Review this document for coherence. Identify gaps. Ask me the clarifying questions you need answered before anything gets written.*

This was the moment everything changed.

Because now the AI had real material to work with, it came back with genuinely good questions — questions I hadn't considered. It identified a gap in how I'd thought about the RSU perquisite tax flow. It flagged that I'd described two conflicting behaviours for what should happen to an FD holding at maturity. It asked how family accounts should behave if a family owner deletes their account.

I answered all of those questions. Then I asked it to produce a structured PRD — Product Requirements Document.

The PRD it produced was good. Not because the AI was clever, but because **I had given it the thinking it needed to be useful.**

---

## The Three-Document Rule

One of the clearest things to emerge from the planning phase was the discipline of keeping three types of documents strictly separate.

**The PRD** — what the product does, who it's for, what each phase covers. No library names. No schema decisions. No code patterns.

**The Architecture Document** — database schema, data models, transaction flows, technical decisions. No product strategy. No user personas.

**Sprint Design Documents** — implementation plan for each sprint, test scenarios, acceptance criteria. No architectural philosophy. No product vision.

The temptation is to blend these. To put a bit of architecture into the PRD because it's relevant. To explain product rationale in the sprint doc because it provides context.

Don't. Once documents blur, every reference becomes polluted. The AI reads the architecture document and starts making product decisions. It reads the sprint doc and starts re-litigating architectural choices that were already settled.

> *Each document has one job. Keep it clean.*

The AI itself recognised that the implementation scope was too large for a single sprint design document and broke it into eight separate sprint design documents — Sprints 0 through 7 in the original plan. This was the right call, and I hadn't asked it to do it. But it was only able to make that judgement because the planning documents were clear enough that it could see the scope accurately.

---

## The Iteration Rule

Once the PRD was drafted, I didn't move on. I asked the AI to review it against the architecture document and flag gaps.

It found gaps.

After both documents were refined, I asked it to review them together. More gaps — smaller this time.

After the third pass, there was one minor inconsistency in how I'd described the tax classification for SGB bonds held to maturity vs. sold early. That was it.

Three passes. Not one, not ten. **Two to three rounds of iteration per stage is the sweet spot.** One pass leaves too many errors. Infinite passes waste time and degrade the AI's context with diminishing returns. Two or three passes done thoughtfully catches most of what matters.

The other rule: **refine early, at every stage. Not all at once at the end.**

If you wait until five sprints are implemented to do your first serious review pass, the AI has to hold enormous context to identify issues. If you refine at each stage — after the PRD, after the architecture, after each sprint design — the AI is working with manageable scope and errors stay small and cheap to fix.

---

## Phase 2 and 3: Plan Loosely on Purpose

The PRD covered Phases 1, 2, and 3. But I asked the AI to draft Phase 2 and 3 as **lightweight guidelines** — not rigid specifications.

The instruction was explicit: *don't be rigid, because Phase 1 development may change some of these decisions.*

This was the right call. Some Phase 1 decisions shifted during implementation. Having Phase 2 and 3 as loose direction meant there was nothing to undo when they shifted. No specifications to re-write, no architectural commitments to walk back.

Phase 1 code includes hooks for Phase 2 features — for example, every tax handler accepts an `as_of_date` parameter, which Phase 1 defaults to today and Phase 2 will use to run "what if I sell on this date" simulations. That hook was built deliberately. But the Phase 2 spec that will use it is still loose, because I haven't built it yet and I don't yet know exactly what I'll need.

---

## The Principle, Applied

Here is what I would tell anyone starting an AI-assisted build:

The AI's output quality is a direct function of your input quality. This is not a flaw. It is the nature of the tool.

Your job — before you open the AI — is to think. Brainstorm freely, then filter ruthlessly. Write user journeys for every meaningful flow. Build complete documents before asking for any implementation. Ask the AI to evaluate and challenge before it builds.

None of this is complicated. All of it is effortful.

> *You have to clear your own head before you can direct an AI. Think of it as a powerful executor — completely dependent on your guidance.*

The extraordinary thing is that this principle makes you better at product work regardless of whether AI is involved. The discipline of full user journeys, clear documents, and iterative review isn't an AI technique. It's just good product thinking. AI makes the cost of skipping it much higher than it used to be — because AI can build wrong things very quickly.

## 📌 Title: *The Architecture Decision That Makes My Finance App Correct Forever*

*Part 3 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

Here is a bug pattern that will be familiar to anyone who has built software that stores state.

You have a table that tracks something derived — a running total, a computed balance, a portfolio value. A transaction creates or updates the derived value. Another transaction updates it again. Over time, the derived value drifts. An edge case wasn't handled. A transaction was edited. A row was deleted without updating the derived state. Now the numbers are wrong, in a way that is invisible unless you manually recompute everything and compare.

In a personal finance application — one designed to track my family's net worth across 11 asset classes for the next fifty years — this is not an acceptable bug. I cannot have a number in my dashboard that is subtly, silently wrong.

The solution is not better bug fixing. It's an architectural decision that makes the bug impossible.

---

## Transactions Are the Source of Truth. Always.

The core principle, stated simply: **every financial event is recorded as a transaction. Holdings are always computed from the transaction history. Holdings are never stored independently.**

What this means in practice:

- **Edit a past transaction** → the holding automatically recomputes
- **Delete a transaction** → the holding adjusts
- **Add a corporate action** → the holding recomputes from scratch, incorporating the new event
- **Drop the entire holdings table** → rebuild it completely from transactions, losing nothing

The holdings summary table is a **cache**, not a source of truth. It exists for dashboard speed. It can be nuked and rebuilt in seconds. No data lives there that doesn't already exist in the transaction history.

This eliminates an entire class of bugs. Not reduces — eliminates. There is no code path by which a holding can become "out of sync" with reality, because there is no independent holding state to go out of sync.

For a financial application where accuracy over decades matters, this is non-negotiable. The complexity cost is real — every operation triggers a recomputation — but the reliability benefit compounds over years of use.

> *Edit a past transaction → holding automatically recomputes. Delete a transaction → holding adjusts. No data can ever be "out of sync."*

---

## Strict Layer Separation — Every Layer Has One Job

The second architectural decision that paid consistent dividends: absolute, enforced separation between layers.

**Routes** accept HTTP requests and return HTML. They do not compute anything.

**Services** contain all business logic, validation, and orchestration. This is the only place decisions are made.

**Repositories** read and write the database. They make no decisions and perform no calculations.

**Templates** render HTML. They display data they are given. They never calculate values.

This sounds textbook. It is textbook. And the discipline of maintaining it paid off more times than I can count.

When I needed to add tax simulation in Phase 2, the service layer already contained all the business logic — cleanly separated, fully tested, composable. No logic was buried in a route handler that I'd have to extract. No calculation was happening in a template that I'd have to find and move.

When business logic leaks into routes or templates — which it always does when the separation isn't enforced — new features can't reuse it. Every new requirement becomes a refactor. That debt compounds across phases.

The rule I enforced throughout: **if a route handler is doing anything other than accepting a request and calling a service, something is wrong.**

---

## The Asset Class Handler Pattern

NEESH tracks 11 asset classes. Each has fundamentally different behaviour — different transaction types, different valuation logic, different tax rules, different lifecycle events.

The solution: every asset class implements the same interface.

The same methods. The same signatures. Whether the request is for a stock holding, an RSU vest, an FD maturity, or a mutual fund SIP — the service layer calls the same interface and gets back a consistent response.

Tax rates and thresholds are **class-level constants**, not buried in if/else chains:

- LTCG rate for equity: `0.125` — one line, one place
- STCG threshold for debt mutual funds: `730` days — one line, one place
- SGB: tax-exempt at 8-year maturity — one method, one handler

When the Indian budget changes the LTCG rate — which it did in 2024 — it is a one-line update per handler. Not a code excavation to find everywhere the rate was hardcoded.

The `as_of_date` parameter on every tax handler method was built in Phase 1 for Phase 2's benefit. Phase 1 defaults it to today. Phase 2 will pass future dates to run "what if I sell on this date" simulations. That integration point — zero Phase 2 refactoring required — was a deliberate architectural choice made before Phase 2 scope was even finalised.

---

## SQLite in WAL Mode — Chosen Deliberately, Not By Default

The database choice prompted the most questions when I described the system to engineers. SQLite? Not PostgreSQL?

Yes. Deliberately, with clear reasoning.

**SQLite is the most deployed database in existence.** It is not going away. There is no SQLite server to maintain, no daemon to restart, no connection pool to tune. The entire database is a single `.db` file. Backups are trivial — copy one file. Restoration is trivial — replace one file.

**WAL mode** (Write-Ahead Logging) gives concurrent reads while writes queue. With 2–3 simultaneous family users doing infrequent writes, this is never a bottleneck. The tradeoff only matters at scale. This application will never be at that scale.

**Schema evolution through Alembic migrations.** Every schema change is a numbered, reversible script. No manual `ALTER TABLE`. No undocumented changes. Over fifty years of potential schema evolution, having an auditable migration history is not optional.

**Projected size over 20 years: ~100MB.** SQLite supports 281 TB. This is not a constraint.

The engineers asking about PostgreSQL are solving for problems this application will never have. The right tool is the simplest one that fully solves the actual problem. SQLite solves this one completely.

---

## Two Uniqueness Constraints That Cost Me Hours

These look minor. They were not.

**Transaction duplicate detection** must match on ALL of: `asset_class`, `symbol`, `transaction_date`, `quantity`, `price_per_unit`, and `instrument_name`.

The first version matched on only the first five. The missing field — `instrument_name` — meant duplicate warnings fired for two different SGB series (Sovereign Gold Bond tranches) bought on the same date at the same price. They were distinct instruments. The system thought they were duplicates.

Missing one field from a composite key produces false positives that are invisible until a user reports them. The fix is always the same: match on every distinguishing field. You can legitimately buy the same stock multiple times on the same day at different prices. The duplicate check must be precise enough not to block that.

**Holdings lookup must always filter by platform.**

Holdings are unique by `(user_id, asset_class, symbol, platform)`. Not by symbol alone. You can hold TCS on both Zerodha and Groww. They are separate holdings with separate cost bases and separate XIRR calculations.

The first version of the XIRR calculation only filtered by symbol. It found both TCS holdings and mixed their cash flows. The resulting XIRR was wrong — not obviously wrong, but subtly wrong in a way that only surfaced when I manually cross-checked against individual position returns.

> *Composite keys need to be respected everywhere — not just in the schema definition, but in every query and every lookup.*

---

## Why Architecture Decisions Compound

I want to close with the observation that felt most significant after completing Phase 1.

Architecture decisions are not isolated. Each one creates a constraint that every subsequent decision must work within. A good architectural foundation means new features slot in cleanly. A poor foundation means every new feature involves some amount of excavation.

Transactions as source of truth: I've never had a holdings inconsistency bug. Not one.

Strict layer separation: Phase 2 features compose with existing services without touching routes or templates.

Asset class handler interface: Adding the RSU handler didn't require changes to the existing equity or mutual fund handlers.

SQLite: I've never had a database outage. The Raspberry Pi has rebooted. The database came back exactly as it was.

These weren't lucky outcomes. They were the direct results of specific decisions made during planning — before any code was written, when those decisions were still cheap to change.

The right time to get architecture right is before you start building it.

## 📌 Title: *Why I Used Three Different AI Models to Parse One Excel File*

*Part 4 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

Let me describe the import problem honestly.

My family has been investing for years. That means years of Zerodha tradebook entries — stocks bought and sold, dividends received, bonus shares from corporate actions. Years of CAMS statements for mutual funds. Fixed deposit receipts across multiple banks. RSU vest confirmations in USD. All of it sitting in different files, in different formats, from different institutions.

Manually entering every transaction into a new system was never an option. Even if I had the patience — which I don't — manual entry introduces errors. Missed transactions, wrong prices, inverted buy/sell labels. A financial system is only as accurate as its input data.

So the question wasn't whether to build an AI import pipeline. The question was how to build one that was actually accurate.

The answer turned out to be: use three different models, in sequence, each doing a different job.

---

## Why One Model Isn't Enough

The naive approach: send the file to a large language model, ask it to extract transactions, trust the output.

This doesn't work reliably enough for financial data. Not because the models are bad — they're genuinely impressive — but because extraction and verification are fundamentally different tasks, and doing both in one pass introduces a category of errors that are hard to detect.

The errors I saw before adding the verification step:

- **Wrong transaction types** — a dividend entry labelled as a buy. Visually similar rows, different meaning.
- **Inverted amount direction** — a sale amount shown as a positive purchase cost
- **Currency confusion** — RSU vest values in USD, interpreted as INR
- **Date format misreads** — `03/04/25` interpreted as March 4th instead of April 3rd in an Indian context

None of these are catastrophic individually. But in a financial system, "close enough" is not acceptable. Each error cascades — wrong cost basis, wrong XIRR, wrong tax classification.

The solution isn't a smarter single model. It's a pipeline where each stage has a specific, narrower job.

---

## Tier 1 — The Local Regex Parser

For known file formats, AI is overkill.

Zerodha's tradebook CSV has a consistent structure. CAMS statements follow a known pattern. NSDL CAS statements have a defined format. For these, a local regex parser — written once, runs instantly, costs nothing — handles extraction reliably.

**Speed:** Immediate. No API call, no network latency.
**Cost:** Zero. No tokens consumed.
**Reliability:** 100% for known formats — no model reasoning involved, just pattern matching.

If the file matches a known format, Tier 1 handles it and the pipeline stops. The AI tiers only activate when the file format isn't recognised.

This matters because the most common file in practice — a standard Zerodha tradebook — never touches an AI model at all. The AI is reserved for the exception, not the rule.

---

## Tier 2 — Gemini Flash (Extraction)

For unknown formats, Gemini Flash handles extraction.

Flash is fast. It's designed for structured data tasks. Given a file's content as text, it reliably identifies transaction rows, maps columns to fields, and returns a structured JSON response.

**A key implementation decision:** Excel and CSV files are parsed locally first — using `openpyxl` and Python's `csv` module — and sent to Gemini as **plain text**, not as binary files.

This avoids Gemini's File API quotas entirely. It also improves reliability — models reason better over clean text than over opaque binary formats. A 500-row CSV becomes a text block that Flash can scan systematically.

The prompt specifies exactly what fields to extract: `symbol`, `transaction_type`, `quantity`, `price`, `date`, `currency`, `platform`. It also specifies what to do with ambiguous cases — err towards inclusion, flag uncertainty, never drop rows silently.

**Speed:** 2–3 seconds per file.
**Cost:** Free tier (15 RPM limit, more than sufficient).

---

## Tier 3 — Gemini Pro (Verification)

This is the step that most import pipelines skip. It is the step that justified the entire pipeline's complexity.

After Flash extracts the transactions, Pro re-reads the original file content alongside the extracted JSON and asks a specific question: *does this extraction accurately represent what's in the file?*

It is not re-extracting. It is verifying. Checking for field mismatches, transaction type errors, currency confusion, date format inconsistencies.

In testing against real broker statements: **the Pro verification step caught approximately 15% of extraction errors** that Flash had made. Not because Flash is unreliable — it's an excellent extraction model — but because verification is a reasoning task, not an extraction task. Pro's reasoning capability is what catches the subtle errors: the dividend labelled as a buy, the inverted sale amount.

> *Flash extracts. Pro verifies. The verification step caught 15% of errors that extraction missed.*

**Speed:** 5–7 seconds.
**Cost:** Free tier (2 RPM limit — the bottleneck, handled by tiering).

---

## The LiteLLM Fallback

When Gemini is unavailable or rate-limited, the pipeline falls back to LiteLLM — an OpenAI-compatible proxy that routes to any backend model.

This was not hypothetical. During development, I used a corporate internal LLM gateway that was OpenAI-compatible. LiteLLM made that a configuration change, not a code change. Services call `get_ai_provider(type)` and never import provider code directly — the backend is fully swappable.

The fallback exists because a production financial system cannot silently fail when the primary AI provider has an outage. The user should always be able to import their statements. The fallback ensures that.

---

## The Design Decisions That Matter Most

Three decisions shaped the import pipeline more than the model choice:

**1. The user reviews everything. Always.**

All extracted transactions are shown for review before any import. Every row is selected by default. Nothing is silently dropped based on confidence scores. No AI confidence threshold determines what the user sees.

This is intentional. In a financial system, the cost of a missed transaction — one that the AI silently discarded because it wasn't confident — is higher than the cost of the user reviewing an extra row. The user is always the final check.

**2. Files go in as text, not binary.**

Sending files through Gemini's File API introduces quota constraints and binary handling complexity. Parsing locally and sending as structured text is faster, more reliable, and quota-free.

**3. Broker statements are ground truth.**

If a file contains a transaction that looks impossible — a sell for more shares than the system thinks you own — that doesn't mean the file is wrong. It usually means a corporate action hasn't been applied yet. The import pipeline never blocks based on apparent impossibility. It imports with a warning and lets the user resolve the underlying cause.

---

## The Same Principle, Applied at the Product Level

Here is the meta-observation that I find most interesting about this pipeline.

The three-tier model selection — right model for the right task — is the same principle I wrote about for AI-assisted development. Flash for extraction. Pro for verification. Don't use Pro for tasks that Flash handles well. Don't use Flash for tasks that require deeper reasoning.

NEESH's import pipeline didn't happen to use this principle. It was designed to use it, because the principle emerged from building the product itself.

> *The goal is not to always use the best model. The goal is to always use the right model.*

When you internalize that principle, it changes how you evaluate every AI-assisted task — whether you're parsing a broker statement, reviewing a PRD, or deciding which model to use for a code implementation.

## 📌 Title: *The Most Important Skill in AI-Assisted Development Isn't Coding*

*Part 5 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

Cover image: **`neesh_post5_cover.png`** in your NEESH folder.

---

I want to tell you about the single most useful thing I learned over the course of building this application. It is not a technical insight. It is not about architecture or model selection or test strategy. It is something that sounds almost embarrassingly obvious once stated — and that I completely underestimated before this project forced me to take it seriously.

The most important skill in AI-assisted development is knowing how to describe precisely what you observe and what you expect.

That's it. That's the skill.

Let me show you what that means in practice, because the gap between "I know that" and "I actually do that" is wider than it sounds.

---

## The Two Versions of the Same Feedback

**Vague version:** *"The date field doesn't work properly."*

**Specific version:** *"There is a calendar icon inside the date input field. Clicking the calendar icon opens the date picker. However, clicking anywhere else on the input field does not open the date picker. Expected: clicking anywhere on the input field should open the date picker, not just the icon."*

The vague version leaves the AI guessing. It might fix the wrong thing. It might fix the right thing partially. It might ask you four clarifying questions before it can proceed. Every round of that back-and-forth costs you context, time, and momentum.

The specific version gives the AI everything it needs to act immediately and correctly. First try.

> *Vague feedback produces plausible-sounding-but-wrong fixes. Specific feedback produces correct ones.*

The difference in outcome is not marginal. Over a project spanning 18 sprints, the compounding effect of consistently specific feedback versus consistently vague feedback is the difference between a project that makes clean forward progress and one that constantly revisits the same ground.

---

## The Bug Report Format

I learned to structure every piece of feedback like a product bug report. Not because I was writing formal documentation — but because that format forces precision.

**What I did:** I navigated to the equity holdings page and clicked on the TCS row.

**What happened:** The page refreshed and navigated me back to the dashboard instead of opening the TCS detail view.

**What I expected:** Clicking a holdings row should open a detail view for that holding, showing transaction history, XIRR, tax classification, and lot breakdown.

**Context:** This happens consistently on the equity holdings page. The mutual funds page works correctly — clicking a row opens the detail view as expected.

Four components. None optional. When I gave the AI all four, it fixed things correctly without follow-up questions. When I gave it fewer, it guessed — and its guesses were sometimes right, sometimes adjacent, occasionally in the wrong direction entirely.

---

## The Batch Size Rule

The second part of the feedback discipline: **3 to 4 specific points per cycle. Never 10.**

I learned this one painfully. I'd accumulate observations across a testing session and then send them all at once — a 10-point list, each item specific, each item valid. The AI would work through them. Some got fixed. Some got partially addressed. Some got forgotten by the time the AI reached item 9. By item 7, the context had shifted enough that the AI was no longer tracking the full list accurately.

The fix is simple: break it up. Send 3–4 specific points. Wait for implementation. Verify. Then send the next batch. This structure, applied consistently, reduced the back-and-forth dramatically and improved the hit rate from the first implementation pass.

> *10 points at once exceeds what the AI can reliably track end-to-end before the context shifts. 3–4 points is the practical limit for a clean implementation cycle.*

---

## The Context Window Is the Most Important Constraint You're Not Designing Around

If there is one technical property of AI systems that anyone building seriously with them must understand, it is the context window.

The AI holds a limited amount of information at once. As conversations grow longer — as documents get larger, as code accumulates, as feedback cycles multiply — the AI starts to forget things. Not metaphorically. Literally. Earlier parts of the conversation fall outside the context window and the AI has no access to them.

This is not a flaw you can fix. It is a property of the tool you have to design around.

Here is how it affected the build:

- **Documents were incomplete** because the AI ran out of context while generating them. I'd ask it to review its own output and it would find gaps it had no memory of creating.
- **Feedback I gave was only partially implemented** because a 10-point list exceeded what the AI could track end-to-end before the window shifted.
- **Architecture decisions made early in the conversation were forgotten** by the time implementation sprints began.

---

## Four Strategies That Helped

**Keep documents as the source of truth, not the conversation.**

Every decision goes into a document. Not into a chat message. The AI references documents. When context is lost, you point it back to the document. The document doesn't forget.

**Break feedback into small batches.**

Covered above. 3–4 points. Verify. Then next batch.

**After each sprint, explicitly ask the AI to update documents.**

Don't assume it remembers to do this. It doesn't. The instruction "after implementing, update the architecture doc and sprint design to reflect what changed" needs to be explicit every single time.

**Refine documents iteratively, at every stage.**

Don't defer all review to the end. Review the PRD when it's done. Review the architecture doc when it's done. Each stage gets its own review pass. By the time you're in sprint 10, you can't hold enough context to meaningfully review sprint 1's design document. Do it when it's fresh.

---

## Git After Every Sprint — The Safety Net You Must Have

One more point that belongs in this post because it's about protecting the quality of your work from the AI — and from yourself.

When an AI coding agent writes, modifies, and sometimes deletes files at speed — across multiple files in a single operation — you are not watching every individual change. You are reviewing outcomes. This is fine when things go right. When things go wrong, you need a way back.

Two scenarios that make this concrete:

**The wrong prompt.** You give the AI an instruction that's slightly off. It interprets it in a way you didn't intend and starts rewriting things that were working. By the time you notice, it's touched six files.

**System failure.** Your machine crashes. The session terminates mid-implementation. Hours of AI-generated code, gone.

Both have the same solution: **Git from day one. Commit after every sprint.**

I went further: I configured Git hooks to block the AI from running destructive commands — `git commit`, `git push`, `git rm`, `git reset`. The AI can view status, diffs, and logs. It cannot push, commit, or discard. I control what gets committed. This single guardrail prevented at least two incidents where the AI would have committed incomplete work.

> *AI writes code fast. Mistakes happen fast too. Git ensures that fast mistakes don't become permanent ones.*

---

## The Underlying Principle

Here is why I think this skill — precise description of observations and expectations — matters more than coding ability in AI-assisted development.

Coding ability helps you evaluate what the AI produced and identify whether it's correct. That's valuable. But it happens after the AI has already done the work.

Communication precision determines the quality of what the AI produces in the first place. It happens before. It shapes every implementation, every fix, every sprint.

If you write vague prompts and vague feedback, you will spend the majority of your time in correction loops — iterating toward something you could have described precisely at the start.

If you write specific prompts and specific feedback, you spend the majority of your time moving forward.

That is the entire practical difference between the two approaches.

## 📌 Title: *You're Probably Using AI Wrong — And Paying Too Much For It*

*Part 6 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

Cover image: **`neesh_post6_cover.png`** in your NEESH folder.

---

Building this application was not cheap. Not in time, and not in money.

I want to talk about the cost dimension honestly, because it's one of the least discussed practical realities of working with AI tools — especially when you're building something substantial rather than running occasional queries.

The total cost came from two sources: the AI tools I used for planning and document review, and the AI coding agent that implemented the sprints. Over eighteen sprints, those costs added up in ways I hadn't anticipated at the start.

And the most expensive mistakes weren't the ones where AI tools failed to deliver quality. They were the ones where I used the wrong tool for the job entirely — paying a premium rate for work that didn't require it.

---

## There Is a Wide Spectrum of AI Models

This sounds obvious. In practice, it is widely ignored.

The AI landscape in 2025 isn't one thing. It's a spectrum — from lightweight, fast, cheap models that are excellent at simple structured tasks, to heavyweight reasoning models that are genuinely worth their cost for complex analysis and deep thinking.

These models differ in four meaningful dimensions:
- **Capability** — what they can reliably do well
- **Speed** — how fast they respond
- **Context window** — how much they can hold at once
- **Cost per token** — what you pay for each request

Using a high-capability reasoning model for a task that doesn't require it is like hiring a senior architect to write meeting notes. The notes will be excellent. You have massively overpaid. The architect has wasted time on work that didn't need their expertise.

AI models work exactly the same way.

---

## The Mental Model I Now Use

Before starting any AI-assisted task, I ask four questions:

**1. What is the actual complexity of what I'm asking?**
Is this a task that requires deep reasoning and judgement — or is it clear, structured execution?

**2. Does this require thinking, or doing?**
Reviewing a PRD for logical gaps requires thinking. Re-formatting a document that's already been approved requires doing.

**3. What context window does this task need?**
A large, complex document needs a model with sufficient context to hold it all at once. A simple grammar check does not.

**4. What does it cost if the output is mediocre?**
If the output being slightly off means hours of debugging or a wrong architectural decision, use the capable model. If the output being slightly off means a mildly awkward sentence that you'll proofread anyway, use the lighter one.

---

## The Analogy That Makes This Concrete

Imagine you need to both design a bridge and paint it.

You hire a structural engineer to design it — because the design requires deep expertise, and the cost of a wrong decision is catastrophic. A miscalculation here means the bridge fails.

Once the design is complete and approved, you hire a painter to paint it. You do not pay the structural engineer's day rate to apply paint. The painting doesn't require structural expertise. It requires painting skill, which the painter has at a fraction of the cost.

> *AI models are the same. Don't pay a structural engineer's rate to paint.*

In practice:

**Use high-capability models for:**
- Reviewing documents for logical gaps and contradictions
- Architecture decisions that require understanding multiple competing constraints
- Generating code for complex, non-obvious logic
- Identifying edge cases in financial rules or business requirements

**Use lighter, faster models for:**
- Grammar and phrasing cleanup on already-approved content
- Reformatting structured data that's already correct
- Summarisation of clear, well-structured input
- Simple factual questions with unambiguous answers

---

## What This Cost Me When I Got It Wrong

The pattern that cost the most: using a high-capability model for tasks that were really just execution.

Once a sprint design document was approved and finalized, any further refinements — phrasing improvements, minor restructuring, cleanup — didn't need a reasoning model. The thinking was done. What remained was execution. Using a heavyweight model for that is real money spent on marginal value.

The pattern that cost the most in a different way: using a lighter model for tasks that actually required reasoning.

Asking a lightweight model to identify gaps in a complex architecture document — gaps that require understanding how Phase 1 components interact with Phase 2 plans — produced confident-sounding output that missed things. The errors looked plausible. Some made it further into development than they should have before surfacing.

Both mistakes are real. The first wastes money. The second wastes time. **Time is more expensive than money on a personal project.**

---

## NEESH's Own AI Pipeline Is Built On This Principle

Here is what I find most satisfying about this lesson: NEESH's import pipeline embodies it at the product level.

The three-tier parsing pipeline applies exactly the same logic:

- **Local regex parser** — for known formats. Zero AI cost because no AI reasoning is required.
- **Gemini Flash** — for extraction from unknown formats. Fast, cheap, excellent at the specific task of pulling structured data from unstructured text.
- **Gemini Pro** — for verification. Slower, more expensive, applied only to the verification step that actually requires reasoning about whether the extracted data makes sense.

Pro is not used for extraction because Flash does that well at lower cost and latency. Flash is not used for verification because it produces confident-but-wrong outputs on the reasoning step. Each model does the job it is suited for.

The verification step costs more per file than the extraction step. It's worth it — it catches approximately 15% of extraction errors. But using Pro for everything would cost roughly 8x more per import with no improvement in extraction quality.

> *The goal is not to always use the best model. The goal is to always use the right model.*

---

## A Practical Starting Point

If you're building something with AI and haven't thought about model selection deliberately, here is where to start:

Make a list of the AI-assisted tasks in your project. For each one, honestly classify it: does this require reasoning, or execution? Does the cost of a mediocre output justify a more capable model?

You'll find that the distribution is probably not what you assumed. Most tasks are execution. A minority require genuine reasoning. Match accordingly, and your costs will drop while your quality on the reasoning tasks — where it actually matters — will stay high or improve.

The most expensive AI tool is the one you're using for everything regardless of fit. The cheapest is the one you're using precisely.

## 📌 Title: *The Feature That Rewrites Everything: Stock Splits, Mergers & Demergers in a Finance App*

*Part 7 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

Cover image: **`neesh_post7_cover.png`** in your NEESH folder.

---

I want to start with a simple question that turned out to be the most complex problem in this entire project.

*"If I import five years of my Zerodha tradebook, will my holdings be accurate?"*

The answer, before corporate action handling was implemented: no. Often significantly wrong.

Not because the import pipeline was broken. Not because the transaction data was incorrect. But because five years of an equity portfolio contains stock splits, bonus issues, demergers, and mergers — and every one of those events, if not handled correctly and in the right order, corrupts every return calculation, every cost basis, and every tax classification that depends on it.

Corporate actions are not an edge case. They are a routine part of owning equities in India. A Zerodha portfolio that's been active for five years likely contains dozens of them. And getting them wrong is invisible — the numbers look plausible, but they're wrong.

---

## What Corporate Actions Actually Are

When a company does a stock split, existing shareholders receive additional shares and the price adjusts proportionally. A 2:1 split doubles your shares at half the price — net worth unchanged, but quantity doubled.

When a company declares a bonus issue, additional shares are given to existing shareholders at zero cost. A 1:2 bonus on 100 shares gives you 50 more shares for free.

When a company demerges a division, shareholders receive shares in the newly independent entity. The cost basis of those shares comes from the original holding, not from a fresh purchase.

When companies merge, shares of the target company are swapped for shares of the acquirer at a defined ratio.

Each of these events changes the quantity of shares in a holding, changes the average cost per share, and changes the holding period for tax purposes. If any of them is applied in the wrong order, or not applied at all, everything downstream is wrong.

---

## Why I Chose NSE as the Data Source

Three options existed: the NSE Corporate Actions API, third-party aggregators like Screener or Investing.com, or manual curation by the admin.

NSE is the authoritative source for Indian equity corporate actions. It's free. It covers bonus issues, splits, symbol changes, demergers, and mergers — everything relevant to a portfolio of Indian equities. The only subtlety: NSE requires specific request headers (`Referer: https://www.nseindia.com/` and a proper User-Agent) or it silently blocks responses. Once that was understood, the integration was straightforward.

Third-party aggregators are convenient until they're not. They can have stale data, missing events, or inconsistent coverage. For a financial application where accuracy over decades matters:

> *Always use the authoritative data source. Third-party aggregators are convenient until they aren't.*

---

## Why Corporate Actions Must Be User-Approved — Never Auto-Applied

The first design question was whether to auto-apply detected corporate actions.

The answer is definitively no — and the reasoning matters.

Auto-applying a bonus issue looks correct in the holdings summary. But what if the NSE API returned the wrong ratio? What if there was a symbol collision and the action got applied to the wrong holding? In a financial system, silent mutations are catastrophic. They produce wrong averages, wrong XIRR, wrong tax classifications — with no visible indication that anything changed.

The correct design: NSE data is fetched daily at 6 AM IST → corporate actions matching user holdings are detected → a pending `UserAction` is created → the user reviews it on the Actions & Notifications page → the user clicks Apply → only then does the system update the holding.

Consumed transactions are soft-marked rather than deleted — the full audit trail is preserved. For an application designed to last fifty years, being able to trace a cost-basis decision back a decade must always be possible.

> *In financial software, no system should silently mutate data. Make every significant change visible, explicit, and reversible.*

---

## The Import Problem I Didn't See Coming

Zerodha's tradebook includes entries for demerger allocations that look like this:

```
2025-10-14,  TMLCV,  1000,  0,  buy
2025-10-14,  TMLPV,  1000,  0,  buy
```

These are shares received when TATA Motors demerged its commercial vehicle division. The buy price is zero because the cost basis comes from the original TATAMOTORS holding — not a fresh purchase.

Without specific handling, these import as transactions with zero cost basis. The resulting holdings have no valid cost. XIRR calculations become meaningless. Tax classification is wrong. And the user has no indication that anything is incorrect — everything looks imported successfully.

The import pipeline needs to detect this pattern: zero-cost buy entries that are corporate action allocations, not purchases. And it needs to find the parent transaction — the original TATAMOTORS holding — to correctly assign cost basis to the child shares.

---

## Why Chronological Order Is Mandatory for Historical Import

The natural instinct when importing five years of data: break it into yearly files. Smaller, easier to review.

This instinct is wrong.

Demerger detection needs to find the parent transaction and the child transaction together. The TATAMOTORS buy was in 2022. The TMLCV demerger entries appear in 2025.

With a year-by-year import:
- **2022 batch:** TATAMOTORS buy is stored in the database ✓
- **2025 batch:** TMLCV zero-cost entries arrive — the pipeline looks for the parent in the *current batch*, not in the database. Detection fails. Incorrect zero-cost transactions are imported. ✗

**The only safe approach for initial historical backfill: import the full multi-year file as a single batch, chronologically.** File size is not a practical concern — five years of active trading is typically 500–3,000 CSV rows, well under 500 KB.

> *For financial data import, completeness beats convenience. One clean chronological import beats multiple partial ones.*

After the initial import, incremental imports are safe — the hybrid detection pattern handles edge cases, and historical holdings already have the correct cost basis.

---

## Hybrid Detection: Making Incremental Imports Safe

Six months after the initial import, you import a new batch. The batch contains more TMLCV entries. The parent TATAMOTORS transaction is in the database — not in this batch. Without modification, detection fails.

The fix: **hybrid detection** — check both the import batch and the existing database for parent symbols. If not found in the batch, query the database. If found, use it to construct the correct transformation.

A companion guard prevents double-application: before any transformation, check whether the corporate action is already marked as applied. If yes, filter the zero-cost entries without re-transforming.

This makes the import system both forward-looking (full batch detection for historical imports) and backward-looking (database lookup for incremental imports).

---

## CA Ordering Enforcement: A Correctness Problem, Not a UX Problem

If a user has three pending corporate actions for a single stock — a 2021 bonus, a 2022 bonus, and a 2023 split — and applies the 2023 split first, every quantity and average price calculation is wrong. The subsequent 2021 and 2022 applications will compound the error.

My first approach: sort the Actions page by ex-date so the oldest appears first. Surely users would apply them in order.

They didn't. The UI doesn't prevent out-of-order application. The consequences are invisible — the numbers look plausible but wrong.

The correct solution has two mandatory parts:

**Server-side guard:** Before applying any corporate action, check if earlier unapplied actions exist for the same symbol. If yes, reject with a specific error: *"Apply the POWERGRID 2021-07-29 bonus first."* The server-side guard is non-negotiable. The UI can be tricked or bypassed. The server cannot.

**UI enforcement:** Disable the Apply button with a tooltip explaining the ordering constraint. Make it visible, not just enforced.

> *Correctness constraints belong on the server, not just the UI. The UI can be bypassed. The server guard cannot.*

---

## The Ex-Date Rule: One Character That Changes Everything

The ex-date is the date on which the stock price adjusts to reflect the corporate action. To receive bonus shares from a bonus issue, you must have bought **before** the ex-date.

Buying **on** the ex-date means you paid the post-adjustment price. You receive no extra shares. The economics are neutral — you simply paid the adjusted price.

```python
# WRONG — includes buyers on the ex-date
eligible_qty = sum(t.quantity for t in buys if t.date <= ex_date)

# CORRECT — excludes buyers on the ex-date
eligible_qty = sum(t.quantity for t in buys if t.date < ex_date)
```

One character. `<=` versus `<`. This single difference changes which lots are eligible for every corporate action calculation across every user's portfolio.

---

## Real-World Verification: VBL

All of the above came together in a verification exercise using a real holding in VBL (Varun Beverages).

VBL had four corporate actions between 2021 and 2024:
- Bonus 1:2 — ex-date 2021-06-10
- Bonus 1:2 — ex-date 2022-06-06
- Split 1:2 — ex-date 2023-06-15
- Split 2:5 — ex-date 2024-09-12

A holding bought **on** the 2021-06-10 ex-date correctly received:
- Zero shares from the 2021 bonus (bought on ex-date — not eligible)
- 3 shares from the 2022 bonus (7 × ½ = 3.5 → floored to 3, cash for the 0.5 fractional)
- 2× quantity from the 2023 split
- 2.5× quantity from the 2024 split

Final quantity matched Zerodha's holdings statement exactly — to the share.

> *"Tests pass" and "matches the broker" are different bars. Verify corporate action logic against real broker data before trusting it.*

That match — not just clean unit tests, but agreement with the actual broker statement — is what correct corporate action implementation looks like.

## 📌 Title: *AI Value Consumer vs. AI Value Creator — Which One Are You?*

*Part 8 of the Building NEESH series — a technical PM's honest account of building a real financial application with AI tools.*

---

Cover image: **`neesh_post8_cover.png`** in your NEESH folder.

---

I want to close this series with something that goes beyond technical patterns, architecture decisions, and AI feedback loops. It's the thing that, when I look back at the last several months, feels like the most significant shift that happened — not in what I built, but in how I think about building.

There is a very real sense of urgency around AI right now. A fear that if you don't act immediately, you'll miss something. That fear is understandable. I felt it too when I started this project. It's the reason I started when I did, and it's probably part of the reason you're reading this.

But that urgency, when it becomes panic, produces a particular kind of mistake. I call it the checkbox mistake.

---

## The Checkbox Mistake

The checkbox mistake looks like this: you hear that AI is transforming how software gets built, how products get designed, how companies operate. You feel the pressure to be part of that. So you find the nearest available AI tool, apply it to the nearest available problem, produce some output, and declare success.

*We're using AI now.* Box ticked.

The output might be fine. It might even be good. But you have built nothing. You have developed no skill. You have no foundation to build on. And the moment the wind shifts — a new tool, a new paradigm, a new capability — you have to start over from nothing, because what you built was borrowed capability, not developed understanding.

I've seen this in individuals and in organisations. It's the most common response to a moment of technological transition, and it's the least productive one.

---

## Value Consumer vs. Value Creator

The distinction that changed how I framed this project is between being an **AI value consumer** and an **AI value creator**.

A **value consumer** plugs AI into an existing workflow as a shortcut. They produce slightly better output in slightly less time. They are entirely dependent on what the AI gives them out of the box, and they've made no investment in understanding *why* it works. They are renters, not builders.

A **value creator** does something harder. They think first. They are deliberate about what they're building, how they're building it, and what problem it actually solves. They invest in understanding the tools deeply enough to use them with precision. They build foundations — good documents, clear processes, structured feedback, version control — that survive model upgrades and tool changes.

When the wind shifts, the value consumer starts over. The value creator adapts, because what they built was grounded in real thinking, not borrowed capability.

> *The opportunity is not to use AI. The opportunity is to use AI to build something that matters.*

---

## What "Real Thinking First" Actually Looked Like

In this project, "thinking first" meant:

- Four weeks of planning before a single line of code was written
- Three types of documents, kept strictly separate, each with one job
- Full user journeys for every feature, written before any AI saw a line of specification
- Two to three iteration passes on every document before moving to implementation
- A feedback discipline that treated every AI interaction like a product requirement

None of that is what AI did for me. All of that is what I did before I asked AI to do anything.

The AI then took that foundation and built something real on top of it. Quickly, accurately, across 18 sprints, in a domain (financial software) where the rules are precise and the errors are invisible.

The AI was extraordinary. But the AI was working with extraordinary input. That's not a coincidence.

---

## The Foundation Survives

Here is the most practical argument for the value creator mindset: the foundations you build this way survive.

The architecture decisions in NEESH — transactions as source of truth, strict layer separation, the asset class handler pattern, SQLite in WAL mode — are not Gemini-specific or Claude-specific or locked to any particular AI development tool. They are correct architectural choices for this kind of application. If every AI tool I used today ceased to exist tomorrow, the codebase would still be correct. The architecture would still hold.

The document discipline I developed — PRD, architecture, sprint design, all kept clean and separate — works regardless of which AI I'm directing. The feedback approach — specific, structured, small-batch — works with any reasonably capable model. The git safety habits — commit after every sprint, restrict destructive AI access — work regardless of which coding agent is writing the code.

> *Build foundations that survive tool changes. The thinking outlasts the tools.*

This is what separates the value creator from the checkbox — not the quality of the output, but the durability of what was built and learned.
